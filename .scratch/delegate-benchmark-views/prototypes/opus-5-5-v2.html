Ideas tried in round 2, and why
  • Matrix layout, chip format. Frontier is one matrix: a row for each model on a harness, a column for each classification. It keeps the round 1 chips (hue is the model family, the sigil is the model, the fill is the harness) and draws a stack about 130px wide with a label on each arm, so the slices can be counted at a glance.
  • Green slices answer the effort question. The green slices count up to the effort the classification favours: the lowest effort within 5 percentile points of the best effort of the model. ▶ and a thick border mark what you run. ▶ above the green means you pay for effort that does not rank higher; ▶ below the green means a dearer effort ranks higher.
  • One square for each benchmark. Each arm has one square for each benchmark of the classification, filled when that benchmark measured that effort. Run and measured, run but not measured (dashed border, empty squares) and measured but not run each look different, and hue does not carry it.
  • One ranking table for each classification. A row for each carried lane, a column for each benchmark. Each cell has the score at the effort the lane runs, and a small ladder: one arm for each effort, as long as its percentile. The solid arm is the lane; an outline arm is an effort the board measured that the lane does not run. A line of text names a cheaper effort that ranks as high, or a dearer effort that ranks higher.
  • A classification score with a stated rule. It is the mean percentile across the benchmarks that measured the lane, where the percentile counts all rows on a board. The evidence stays visible: squares count the benchmarks, a grey bar shows the spread between them, and a lane with thin evidence gets ~, a hollow dot and a dashed tier.
  • Tier now against tier here. The ranking is cut into tiers 4 to 1 in the proportions the tiers hold now. Each row shows both tiers, with ▲ or ▼ and the distance where they differ. Divider rows and four cards name the best lane and the best lane per dollar in each tier.
  • Relative cost. The boards do not share a cost basis (per task, whole run), so the cost of a classification is relative: the cost of the lane divided by the median row cost on the same board, as a geometric mean across benchmarks. Sort by classification score, by one benchmark, by cost or by score per dollar.
  • Flat tabs, one grouping to edit. Frontier, then one tab for each classification. The grouping is one CONFIG object at the top of the script; a board is matched by its source and the start of its name. All other names come from the data.
